**Q (mechanism):** Why can a company report a huge accounting loss even when its business is roughly break-even?

**A:** Anthropic sold forward contracts on its own stock to Google and Amazon. When the stock rose, GAAP accounting booked a loss on those derivatives, even though the rise is an economic gain for the holders. The loss is largely a mark-to-market effect, not an operating problem.

**Q (concept):** What is non-GAAP accounting and why is it debated?

**A:** Non-GAAP numbers adjust standard GAAP results to strip out items a company calls one-time or not reflective of recurring earnings. Critics say companies use it to hide real costs, especially when the 'one-time' items repeat every year. Defenders say GAAP can be distorted and that adjusted numbers can help investors.

**Q (mechanism):** Why is a basket of options worth more than an option on a basket?

**A:** Single-stock calls pay off on each stock that rises and lose only the premium on those that fall. An index call nets gains and losses together, so if half the stocks rise and half fall, it may pay nothing. Separate bets capture more upside.

**Q (mechanism):** Why do equity holders and debt holders want different company structures?

**A:** Equity is like a call option on company value, so equity holders gain from volatility and prefer separate risky bets. Debt holders get capped returns and want stability, so they prefer one diversified company with a large balance sheet, which can borrow cheaply for big projects.

**Q (number):** What are the three levels of AI worry described in the episode?

**A:** Level one: current harms like disinformation, deepfakes, and bias. Level two: job displacement that may require universal basic income. Level three: AI that kills everyone, which is hard to hedge financially.

# Primer: Artisanal Human Money Stuff Podcast

This is a loose, funny chat between Matt Levine (who writes Bloomberg's *Money Stuff* column) and guest host Max Abelson, a Bloomberg financial journalist. Katie Greifeld, the usual co-host, is on parental leave, so guests are filling in. The conversation jumps around a lot. Here's the background that helps you keep up.

## The cast and the tone

Matt writes a daily newsletter known for a dry, cheerful, slightly shrugging style. Max is a reporter who likes to poke at Matt's habits, and his wife is about to have their first baby. Expect jokes, tangents, and running gags (like the word "anthropod"). Underneath the jokes, there are real finance ideas.

## Anthropic and its IPO

**Anthropic** is an AI company that makes the Claude chatbot. It was founded by former OpenAI staff, including CEO Dario Amodei. It is known for emphasizing AI safety.

An **IPO** (initial public offering) is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time. Before that, it files a long legal document called a **prospectus**. It lays out the business, the financials, and the **risk factors**. These are lists of everything that could go wrong, written partly to protect the company from lawsuits.

The hosts discuss what Anthropic's prospectus does and doesn't say. It helps to know a few background points:

- **AGI** means "artificial general intelligence," an AI that can do most intellectual work as well as a person. **Superintelligence** means AI far beyond that.
- Dario Amodei has written long public essays about AI's upsides and dangers. One is called "Machines of Loving Grace," which imagines AI curing diseases and creating abundance.
- **Effective altruism (EA)** is a movement that tries to use evidence and math to do the most good with money and effort. Philosopher Peter Singer is an early influence. In the 2010s, many EA-minded people became worried about AI wiping out humanity, and that worry shaped parts of the AI industry.
- **"Doom" probabilities**: in AI circles, people often state their personal odds that AI leads to human extinction.
- **OpenAI** (maker of ChatGPT, led by Sam Altman) is Anthropic's main rival. Elon Musk co-founded OpenAI and later fell out with it. He now runs his own AI company, xAI.

The title wordplay comes from Greek. *Anthropos* means human, so "anthropic" means human-related. **Philanthropy** is giving to do good. **Misanthropy** is disliking humanity.

## Roko's basilisk

This is a thought experiment from online "rationalist" forums, communities that like to reason carefully about AI and the future. The idea is that a future all-powerful AI might punish people who didn't help bring it into existence. It's a bit like **Pascal's wager**, the old argument that you should believe in God because the downside of being wrong is so large. Most people find it silly. It's also a well-known piece of internet lore, partly because it's said to have connected Elon Musk and the musician Grimes. The hosts joke about whether it could count as **securities fraud** (misleading investors to get them to buy stock).

## GAAP, non-GAAP, and the "$80 billion loss"

**GAAP** stands for Generally Accepted Accounting Principles. These are the standard US rulebook for how companies report profits and losses. Standard rules make companies comparable and make cheating harder. But the rules are general, so sometimes they produce numbers that don't match what's economically happening.

So companies often also report **non-GAAP** or "adjusted" numbers. These strip out items the company says are misleading. Skeptics worry this lets companies hide bad news, especially by calling recurring costs "one-time events." Matt's view, which he's explained in his column, is that adjusting isn't automatically dishonest.

In this episode, the issue is that Anthropic reported a huge accounting loss that doesn't seem to reflect real cash trouble. It comes from contracts tied to its own stock, which the hosts explain. The rough ideas you need:

- A **derivative** is a contract whose value depends on something else, like a stock price.
- Anthropic sold agreements to big investors (Google and Amazon are mentioned) involving future delivery of its shares.
- Under accounting rules, if your own stock rises, some of these contracts show up as a *loss* on your books, even though a rising stock price is good news for the company.

## Options, in plain English

The second half hinges on options, so here is the minimum.

A **call option** gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy something at a set price (the **strike price**) later. If the stock ends up above the strike, you profit. If it ends up below, you just don't use the option and lose only what you paid for it (the **premium**). So your loss is capped and your gain isn't.

Two other ideas:

- **An index** is a basket of many stocks, like the S&P 500 (500 large US companies). You can buy options on single stocks or on the whole index.
- A **dispersion trade** is a bet that individual stocks will move very differently from one another, even if the index barely moves.

A less obvious fact: a company's **stock** behaves a lot like an option on the company's total value. Lenders get paid first, and shareholders keep whatever is left over. **Debt** (loans and bonds) is different. Lenders get a fixed payment and don't share in big upside. So stockholders and lenders can want different things from a company's risk-taking.

## Conglomerates

A **conglomerate** is one big company that owns many unrelated businesses. These were popular in the 1960s and 70s, and the pitch was diversification: if one division struggles, another can offset it. Later investors tended to prefer pure, separate companies. Keep that history in mind when the hosts talk about Elon Musk's companies.

## The Musk empire and the Tesla/SpaceX question

Musk runs several companies: **Tesla** (electric cars), **SpaceX** (rockets and satellites), **xAI** (AI), **X** (formerly Twitter), and **Neuralink** (brain implants). According to the hosts, X has been folded into xAI, and xAI into SpaceX. The open question is whether SpaceX and Tesla might also combine.

Some terms that come up:

- **Special committee**: when a controlling shareholder (here, Musk) is on both sides of a deal, independent board members review it. This is meant to protect minority shareholders from being treated unfairly, and to head off lawsuits.
- **Delaware vs. Texas**: most big US companies are incorporated in Delaware, whose courts are famous for policing conflicted deals. Tesla and SpaceX have moved to Texas, which has a newer business court system that hasn't been tested much.
- **Data centers**: AI needs enormous buildings full of chips (mostly from **Nvidia**), which cost tens or hundreds of billions of dollars. Companies often borrow heavily to pay for them.

## Onion futures and prediction markets

A **future** is a contract to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date. Futures are normally legal for many commodities. But in the late 1950s, after traders cornered the onion market (buying up supply to control the price), Congress banned onion futures. Movie box office futures were banned much later, after Hollywood lobbied against them. Both bans still exist.

**Prediction markets** are platforms where people bet on real-world outcomes, such as elections, sports, or even whether a certain word will be said on a broadcast. Matt jokes that people have bet on whether he'll mention things in his column. The hosts also touch on a more serious concern: sports gambling creeping into places like retirement accounts.

## Smaller references

- **Janet Malcolm**: a journalist known for essays arguing that institutions like journalism, law, and psychoanalysis are imperfect ways of finding truth.
- **"Known knowns"**: a phrase from former US Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld about risks you know you know, know you don't know, or don't know you don't know.
- **Transcendental meditation (TM)**: a mantra-based meditation technique associated with filmmaker David Lynch.

You don't need to remember all this. If you know what a call option is, what GAAP is, and why people argue about Musk's companies, you'll be able to follow along and enjoy the jokes.

# Primer: AI Prospectuses, Options Math, and Onions

This episode of the Money Stuff podcast jumps fast between AI companies going public, accounting rules, options theory, Elon Musk's corporate empire, and a 1950s law about onions. The hosts assume you know the basics of each. Here is that background.

## The players

**Matt Levine** writes *Money Stuff*, a popular Bloomberg finance newsletter. He's a former lawyer and investment banker known for a dry, amused tone. **Max Abelson** is a Bloomberg News reporter filling in for Matt's usual co-host, Katie Greifeld, who is on parental leave.

**Anthropic** is an AI company. It makes the Claude chatbot and sells AI to businesses. It was founded in 2021 by people who left **OpenAI**, the maker of ChatGPT. They left partly because they wanted a more safety-focused approach. Anthropic's CEO is **Dario Amodei**. OpenAI's CEO is **Sam Altman**. Elon Musk helped found OpenAI, later fell out with it, and now runs his own AI company, **xAI**. The biggest, most advanced AI companies are often called "frontier labs."

**AGI** means "artificial general intelligence." Roughly, it's AI that can do most mental work as well as a human. "Superintelligence" means AI far smarter than any human.

## Going public: IPOs and prospectuses

An **IPO** (initial public offering) is when a private company first sells shares to the public on a stock exchange. Before it can do that, it must file a **prospectus** with regulators. That's a long legal document describing the business, its finances, and its "risk factors." Risk factors are a list of everything that could go wrong. Lawyers write them defensively, so they tend to sound bland and cautious.

Anthropic's leaders have also written long public essays. The most famous is "Machines of Loving Grace" by Amodei. It's an optimistic vision of AI curing diseases and creating abundance. The hosts compare that tone with the much drier prospectus.

## The AI-worry community

A few ideas come up quickly:

- **Effective altruism (EA)** is a movement about doing the most good per dollar or hour. It's tied to philosopher **Peter Singer**. It started with things like global health charities. Over time, many EA people became focused on the risk that advanced AI could wipe out humanity.
- **Rationalists** are an overlapping online community. Their forums, like LessWrong, discuss logic, probability, and AI risk.
- **P(doom)** is slang for "probability of doom." It's someone's personal estimate of the chance AI causes a catastrophe, like human extinction.
- **Roko's basilisk** is a thought experiment from a rationalist forum in 2010. It imagines a future all-powerful AI that punishes people who knew about it but didn't help create it. It's famous mostly for being weird and unsettling.
- **Pascal's wager** is a 1600s argument for believing in God. Even if God is unlikely, the stakes are infinite, so belief is the "safe bet." People compare the basilisk to it.
- **"The race"** is the idea that labs feel pushed to build powerful AI fast, before a rival (another company, or China) does it first.

**Public benefit corporations** are companies legally allowed to pursue a social mission alongside profit. Anthropic is set up this way.

## Accounting: GAAP vs. non-GAAP

**GAAP** stands for Generally Accepted Accounting Principles. These are the standard US rules for how companies report profits and losses. Standard rules make companies comparable and make fraud harder.

But rules are general. Sometimes they produce numbers that don't match how a business is actually doing. So companies often also report **non-GAAP** or "adjusted" numbers. These strip out items the company argues are misleading, such as **one-time events** like a lawsuit settlement. Critics point out the abuse: some companies have "one-time" charges every single year.

Two more terms:

- **Net loss / net income**: the bottom line, profit or loss after everything.
- **Mark-to-market**: valuing something at its current market price each period, and recording gains or losses as that price moves.

A **derivative** is a contract whose value depends on something else, like a stock price. A **forward** is an agreement to buy something later at a price set today. Here's the key odd idea for this episode. Under some accounting rules, if a company sold investors the right to buy its stock later, and the stock then soars, the company can have to record a *loss*. That's because it is now obliged to hand over valuable shares at the old, cheaper price. Big tech companies like Google and Amazon have invested in Anthropic.

## Options: the core math

A **call option** is the right, but not the obligation, to buy a stock at a set price, called the **strike price**, before some date. If the stock rises above the strike, you profit. If it falls, you just don't use the option. Your payoff is zero, not negative, aside from what you paid for the option, called the **premium**.

A **basket** is a group of stocks bundled together, like an index such as the **S&P 500** (500 big US companies). Now compare two things:

- **A basket of options**: a separate call option on each stock.
- **An option on a basket**: one call option on the whole group's combined value.

The first is worth more. Why? With separate options, the winners pay you and the losers just cost you zero. With one combined option, the losers drag down the winners before you see any payoff. Traders exploit this gap in a strategy called the **dispersion trade**. "Dispersion" means how spread out individual stock moves are.

**Volatility** means how much a price swings. Options become more valuable the more volatile the underlying thing is, because big upside swings pay off and big downside swings are capped at zero.

## Equity as an option

Here's a classic finance insight. Owning a company's stock (its **equity**) is like holding a call option on the company. The strike price is the company's debt. If the company is worth more than it owes, shareholders keep the extra. If it's worth less and goes bankrupt, shareholders lose their investment but nothing more. Lenders take the hit.

That leads to a split:

- **Shareholders** like risk and volatility, because their downside is capped.
- **Lenders (creditors)** dislike volatility. They just want to be repaid. They prefer stable, diversified borrowers.

## Conglomerates and Musk's companies

A **conglomerate** is one company that owns many unrelated businesses. Think 1960s–70s giants that owned everything from hotels to missiles. They were sold as "diversified," but they later fell out of favor with stock investors. Today, a business focused on just one thing is called a **pure play**.

Musk runs several companies: **Tesla** (cars, publicly traded), **SpaceX** (rockets and Starlink satellites, private), **xAI**, **X** (formerly Twitter), and **Neuralink** (brain implants). Over the past couple of years, several of these have been merged together. A Tesla–SpaceX combination has been widely discussed.

AI is extremely **capital intensive**. Building data centers full of **Nvidia** chips costs tens or hundreds of billions of dollars. Much of that money is raised as debt.

## Corporate governance basics

When a **controlling shareholder** stands on both sides of a deal, they have a **conflict of interest**. Musk is a big owner of both Tesla and SpaceX, so a merger between them is exactly that kind of deal. The standard protection is a **special committee**. This is a group of independent board members who negotiate on behalf of the other shareholders.

Most big US companies are incorporated in **Delaware**, whose courts are strict about conflicted deals. After a Delaware judge threw out Musk's huge Tesla pay package in 2024, he moved Tesla's and SpaceX's legal home to **Texas**. Texas has just set up new business courts that haven't yet faced a major test.

## Prediction markets and onions

**Prediction markets** like Kalshi and Polymarket let people bet on real-world outcomes: elections, sports, even which words someone will say. Lately they've spread into sports betting and into products sold through brokerage and retirement-type accounts. "Mention markets" are bets on whether something gets mentioned.

**Futures** are contracts to buy or sell something, like wheat or oil, at a set price on a future date. They're regulated by the **CFTC** (Commodity Futures Trading Commission). The **CME** (Chicago Mercantile Exchange) is the biggest futures exchange.

To **corner** a market means buying up so much of something that you control its price. In the 1950s, traders did this with onions, and prices went haywire. Congress responded with the **Onion Futures Act of 1958**, which bans onion futures. It's still law. Movie box-office futures were later banned too. The **University of Chicago** is famous for its free-market "Chicago school" of economics, which helps explain some of the jokes.
